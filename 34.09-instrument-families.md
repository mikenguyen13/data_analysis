## Instrument Families and Special Cases {#sec-instrument-families}

The preceding sections developed what an instrument has to do: shift the treatment, stay out of
the outcome equation, and move everyone in the same direction. Those are properties of a
research design, not of a variable, and they are argued rather than tested. What follows is the
catalogue.

It opens with a field guide (Section \@ref(sec-workhorse-instruments)) that sketches the
workhorse families in a single row each. The sections after it take the families that carry the
most weight in current applied work and develop each one properly: how the instrument is
constructed, what identification argument it rests on, which diagnostics a referee will expect,
and where the design has been shown to break.

They fall into four groups, presented in this order. The first borrows variation from shocks or
institutions that assign something as if at random: shift-share, recentered, and granular designs
(Sections \@ref(sec-shift-share-iv), \@ref(sec-recentered-iv), and \@ref(sec-granular-iv)),
decision-maker leniency (Section \@ref(sec-examiner-design)), quasi-random advertising timing
(Section \@ref(sec-single-source-tv-iv)), and the statutory rules behind simulated instruments
(Section \@ref(sec-simulated-iv)). The second borrows it from the physical or social world:
geographic proximity (Section \@ref(sec-distance-iv)), weather and wind
(Section \@ref(sec-weather-iv)), genetic variants (Section \@ref(sec-mendelian-randomization)),
and network position (Section \@ref(sec-network-peer-iv)). The third manufactures instruments
internally, out of the data's own structure rather than from outside it: lagged levels in a
dynamic panel (Section \@ref(sec-dynamic-panel-iv)) and higher moments of a mismeasured
regressor (Section \@ref(sec-higher-moment-iv)), both of which trade an external variation
argument for a distributional assumption. Section \@ref(sec-finance-shock-iv) then collects the
constructions specific to corporate finance, which cut across the first three groups. The fourth
group deals with cases that are less about finding an instrument than about what to do when the
usual machinery does not apply: proxy variables (Section \@ref(sec-proxy-variables)), where no
instrument exists but a stand-in for the confounder does, and binary endogenous regressors
(Section \@ref(sec-binary-endogenous-regressors)), where the linear model itself is the problem.

A theme recurs often enough to state in advance. In several of these families the first-stage
statistic is uninformative about validity, and in two of them it is actively misleading, large
precisely when the identifying assumption fails. Strength and validity are separate questions,
and only one of them has a test.

The sections are independent of one another. A reader working on a specific design can go
directly to it.

### A Field Guide to Workhorse Instruments {#sec-workhorse-instruments}

Applied work does not invent a new instrument for every question. A small number of instrument families do most of the work across economics, political science, marketing, and epidemiology, and each family carries its own standard objection, usually raised by the paper that first exploited it and refined by the papers that broke it. Knowing the family is most of the referee report. Table \@ref(tab:iv-workhorse-instruments) collects the ones a reader is most likely to meet.

| Instrument family | Typically instruments for | Canonical use | The objection that follows it everywhere |
|----------------|----------------|----------------|-------------------------|
| Quarter or season of birth | Years of schooling | @angrist1991sources | Weak first stage with many instruments [@bound1995problems], and season of birth is correlated with family background [@buckles2013season] |
| Geographic proximity to a facility | Schooling, hospital care, credit access | @card2001estimating | Proximity is chosen, and it proxies for local labour markets and family resources, so the exclusion argument is about residential sorting |
| Sibling sex composition, twin births | Family size | @angrist1998children, @angrist2010multiple | Sex mix can affect parental investment and outcomes directly, bypassing family size |
| Randomly assigned examiners, judges, caseworkers | Incarceration, disability benefits, bankruptcy protection, consumer credit | @aizer2015juvenile, @dobbie2018effects, @maestas2013does, @dobbie2017consumer | Monotonicity fails when examiners specialise rather than differ in leniency, and examiners may influence outcomes through other decisions [@frandsen2023judging] |
| Shift-share and Bartik designs | Local labour demand, import exposure, immigration | @bartik1991benefits, @autor2013china | Identification comes from the shares or from the shocks, and the two readings imply different assumptions and different standard errors [@goldsmith2020bartik; @borusyak2022quasi; @adao2019shift] |
| Weather and rainfall | Income, conflict, protest turnout, agricultural output | @madestam2013political | Weather moves almost everything, so the same instrument is excluded from one outcome and included in another [@mellon2025rain] |
| Lotteries and randomised encouragement | School attendance, program take-up, treatment receipt | @angrist1996identification, @abdulkadirouglu2017research | Only valid for compliers, and defensible only when noncompliance and attrition are documented |
| Cost shifters and rival product characteristics | Prices in demand systems | @berry1995automobile, @nevo2001measuring | Cost shifters correlate with unobserved quality, and rival characteristics require the competitive structure to be right |
| Prices in other markets | Prices in the focal market | @hausman1996valuation | Demand shocks are common across markets, which is the whole reason prices co-move |
| Idiosyncratic shocks to large units | Aggregate demand, aggregate output | @gabaix2024granular | Requires the granular residual to be free of common factors, which is an assumption about the factor structure |
| Policy changes and compulsory-schooling laws | Schooling, insurance coverage, minimum wages | @angrist1991does | Policy is endogenous to local conditions, and the timing is rarely as exogenous as the narrative suggests |
| Single-source and split-cable advertising exposure | Advertising exposure | @gordon2019comparison | Exposure is targeted, so the instrument has to isolate the part of delivery the advertiser did not choose |

Table: (\#tab:iv-workhorse-instruments) Instrument families that recur across applied literatures, with the canonical application and the objection that typically decides whether a new paper using the family is believed.

Two lessons follow from reading the table as a whole rather than row by row. The first is that the objection is usually about the same thing in every family, which is that the variation the analyst calls exogenous was produced by a process someone chose: where to live, which judge to staff, which market to enter, which policy to pass. The second is the cross-study problem raised in the [Challenges](#sec-instrumental-variables) section. Once a family has been used to instrument dozens of different treatments, the union of those first stages is itself evidence that the instrument has many channels to the outcomes of interest, which is why the catalogue in @mellon2025rain is uncomfortable reading for anyone who has used weather. A new application in an old family needs an exclusion argument that is specific to its own setting, not an appeal to the family's pedigree.
